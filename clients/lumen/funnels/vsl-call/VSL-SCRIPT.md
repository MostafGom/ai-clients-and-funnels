## [0:40] The pattern under the exceptions
> art: dial
Here's the thing I've learned from two thousand three hundred families.
Every baby runs on the same mechanism: a wake window, and a stress hormone that floods in when you miss it.
Miss the window and she's wired. Bedtime takes an hour. And at 2 a.m., between sleep cycles, cortisol wakes her up.
That part is the same for every baby. What's different is how you get the timing right when life gets in the way.
So "different" isn't a dead end. It's an adjustment. Let me show you four.

## [1:40] Twins
> art: moon
Two babies, one window, one exhausted parent. The trap is doing bedtime for both at once and getting neither right.
The adjustment: stagger. One goes down fifteen minutes before the other. Same script, same words, two slots.
Ines had eleven-month-old twins, both with reflux, in one room. Night six, both slept through.

## [2:40] Reflux
> art: moon
Reflux babies get put down later because feeding takes longer, and later means overtired.
The adjustment: the feed moves earlier, before the script, with the upright time your pediatrician recommends built into the window.
Timing fixes most of the fighting. The reflux is still there. The 2 a.m. party isn't.

## [3:30] Daycare naps
> art: log
You don't control the naps, so nights feel random. The trap is keeping bedtime fixed when the nap moved.
The adjustment: bedtime comes from whatever daycare gives you that day. Nap ended at two? Bedtime moves. Nap at four? Bedtime moves.
Kwame and Lila's two-year-old was in daycare with a work trip in week two. Night seven, eleven hours. It held through the trip.

## [4:20] Two kids in one room
> art: moon
The fear is that one wakes the other. The trap is keeping the older one up to avoid it, which breaks the older one's timing.
The adjustment: the older child's bedtime first, then the baby's, with the response plan run in the room, lights off, voice low.
Siblings sleep through more than parents expect. Babies are loud. Sleeping children mostly don't care.

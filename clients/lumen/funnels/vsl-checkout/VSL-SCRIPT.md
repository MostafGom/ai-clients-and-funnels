## [4:00] The forty-minute mechanism
> art: dial
Every baby has a wake window. The number of hours she can be awake before her body hits a limit.
At nine months, it's about three to three and three-quarter hours after her last nap.
Push past the window by even forty minutes and her body does something clever and terrible.
It releases cortisol. The stress hormone. The "stay awake, something's wrong" hormone.
Now she's wired. Bedtime takes an hour of rocking. You finally get her down. You exhale.
But cortisol doesn't leave when she falls asleep. It hangs around. And at 2 a.m., between sleep cycles, it wakes her.
That's the party. Not hunger. Not teeth. Not a habit. Forty minutes of timing.
You've seen the signs and read them backwards. The second wind at seven p.m. The giggling, the hyper, the wide eyes.
That looks like "not tired." It's the opposite. It's cortisol. It's the body saying, "I missed the window, stay up."
And the cruelest part? The later she goes down, the earlier she wakes. Overtired babies wake at five. Well-timed babies sleep till seven.
Naps are the same mechanism in daylight. A thirty-minute nap is a baby who went down overtired and couldn't link her sleep cycles.
So the fix is the opposite of what feels logical. Earlier, not later.
Get the window right, and the bedtime fight mostly disappears. Get it right for a few nights in a row, and the 2 a.m. waking goes with it.
